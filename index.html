About

I study how patterns form and how they break: the stripes in a fingerprint, ripples in sand, the rolls in a heated fluid.

Most of my work sits where differential equations meet data. I build methods that read the structure hidden in simulations, and I use machine learning, especially generative models, to reconstruct and generate physical fields such as turbulent flows, ocean waves and atomic structures.

Before graduate school I spent five years at Nationwide Insurance, first as an actuarial analyst and then as a full-stack developer and technical lead. During the PhD I also did research internships at Los Alamos and Lawrence Livermore national laboratories.

Outside of work you'll usually find me hiking, at the dog park with my dog, playing guitar, or out to dinner with friends.

Research

From knees to dislocations: order parameters from data for the phase grain boundaries of stripe patterns

PhD dissertation, University of Arizona, 2026. Advised by Alan C. Newell and Shankar C. Venkataramani.

When two patches of stripes meet at an angle, they form a boundary. At shallow angles the stripes bend smoothly into a knee. At steep angles the boundary breaks into a periodic row of defects called dislocations. The classical phase theory of stripe patterns describes both extremes well, but not the angles in between, or how a knee turns into dislocations over time.

My dissertation develops ways to read a pattern's hidden variables (the local stripe direction and spacing, the amplitude, and where the defects sit) directly from simulation data, using Riesz and Hilbert transform methods. With those fields in hand, I test the phase theory against simulations of the Swift-Hohenberg equation, identify the equations the extracted fields actually obey, and follow how defects are born at the intermediate angles.

Machine learning for physical systems

3D printing designUniversity of Arizona, 2025 to now

Generative models that propose topology-optimized designs for laser powder bed fusion, combined in a product-of-experts style to balance design specifications against manufacturability. With the Department of Systems and Industrial Engineering.

Atomic interfacesLawrence Livermore, 2025

Generating 3D solid-liquid interfaces in copper from molecular dynamics data with a score-based diffusion model, extending an existing implementation to larger domains and building the data processing and validation around it.

TurbulenceLos Alamos, Center for Nonlinear Studies, 2024

Diffusion models that generate turbulent flows and sharpen coarse ones, with a physical constraint built into the architecture that reduced physical inconsistency by two orders of magnitude.

Tsunami reconstructionLos Alamos, 2023

A transformer that reconstructs full tsunami wavefields from a sparse network of ocean sensors, informed by seafloor depth and the shallow water equations. Published in Geophysical Research Letters.

Medical imagingUniversity of Arizona, 2021 to 2022

A forward solver for magneto-acousto-electric tomography, used to test methods for recovering tissue conductivity. Published in Inverse Problems.

Funding

Fusion imagingFunded internal award

Probabilistic 3D reconstruction for inertial confinement fusion

Uncertainty-aware reconstruction of 3D implosion states from sparse diagnostics. A fellow graduate student and I wrote nearly all of it. It later grew into a multi-institutional proposal with partners at Lawrence Livermore, Los Alamos and the Laboratory for Laser Energetics.

Fusion materialsFunded internal award

Large-scale virtual electron diffraction for bridging simulation and experiment of fusion materials

Graph neural networks that predict electron backscatter diffraction patterns from large atomistic simulations of irradiated tungsten. I pitched the idea and wrote the first draft, then developed it with Marat Latypov (Materials Science and Engineering).
